# What Your Bank App's Fees Are Actually Costing You 

I'll admit I never actually read the fee schedule on any account I've ever opened. I don't think most people do — it's usually a PDF nobody clicks, buried three menus deep, written in a font that seems specifically designed to be skimmed past. So I finally sat down and went through what these fees actually add up to, and honestly some of the numbers surprised me.

he monthly maintenance fee is the one that adds up quietly. The average checking account maintenance fee right now is around $14 a month — which sounds trivial until you realize that's about $167 a year, every year, just for the account existing. The part that actually bothers me: this fee disproportionately hits people who can't consistently keep a minimum balance (usually somewhere around $1,500) to get it waived. So the people with the least cushion are the ones most likely to pay it. Meanwhile, over a third of checking accounts charge no maintenance fee at all, and online-only banks are more than twice as likely to be fee-free as traditional branch banks. If you're paying this fee, it's almost always avoidable by switching accounts, not by changing your behavior.

Overdraft fees are smaller than they used to be, but still not nothing. The average overdraft fee has actually been falling for years — it's around $27-31 per occurrence now, down from fees that used to run as high as $35, mostly because of public pressure and a few big banks (Bank of America famously dropped theirs to $10) leading the shift. But "smaller" doesn't mean "small": if you overdraft twice in a week because you didn't see the first one post yet, that's $60 gone, often over a purchase that was only a few dollars.

The fee that's easiest to just... forget you're paying: out-of-network ATM fees. These have crept up toward $5 a transaction on average now, and unlike the other two, there's no minimum balance trick to avoid it — the only real fix is knowing your bank's ATM network before you're standing at a machine at 11pm needing cash.

Here's the framing that actually changed how I think about this: it's not really about the fee itself, it's about what that money could have been doing instead. $167 a year in maintenance fees, redirected into something that earns even a modest return over ten years, adds up to real money — not because the fee itself is huge, but because it compounds the same way savings does, just in reverse. A fee you don't notice this month is still a fee you're paying every month.

The actual fix takes about ten minutes. Most major banks now publish a fee schedule you can search for online without even logging in — search "[your bank] fee schedule PDF" and skim just the maintenance fee and overdraft sections. If either number surprises you, comparing it against two or three fee-free online accounts (Capital One 360 and SoFi are commonly fee-free ones, though it's worth checking current terms) usually makes the switch decision pretty obvious.

I used to think "avoiding bank fees" was one of those tips that sounds responsible but doesn't actually matter much day to day. Turns out it's one of the few finance tips where the fix is genuinely a one-time, ten-minute task instead of an ongoing habit — which is probably why I'd never gotten around to it.
