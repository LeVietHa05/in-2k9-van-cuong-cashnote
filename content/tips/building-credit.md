# Building Credit From Zero: A Practical Starting Guide 

I remember being genuinely confused by this the first time someone explained it to me: you need credit history to get approved for things, but you need to already have been approved for something to build credit history. It sounds like a trick question. It kind of is, for a lot of people — which is actually the exact problem the alternative credit scoring post in this series was all about, just at the scale of one person instead of a whole country.

So if you're starting completely from zero — no credit card, no loan, nothing with your name on a repayment schedule — here's roughly the order that actually works, based on what I've read and what people who've done it describe.

Start with something designed for exactly this problem. A secured credit card is the most common entry point — you put down a deposit (say $200-500), and that becomes your credit limit. It feels a little silly, since you're basically lending the bank your own money, but the point isn't the credit line, it's that the bank reports your payment behavior to credit bureaus every month. That reporting is the actual product you're buying.

Being an authorized user works too, if you have someone who trusts you. If a parent or older sibling adds you to one of their existing credit cards as an authorized user, their account's history can start showing up on your credit report — sometimes even backdated to when they opened it. You don't even need to use the card. This is honestly the fastest shortcut if it's available to you.

Whatever you start with, the only rule that actually matters is: never miss a payment. Not "pay most of it." Not "pay the minimum most months." Payment history is worth more to your credit score than almost anything else combined, and missed payments stay visible for years. If you're nervous about forgetting, set up autopay for at least the minimum on day one, before you've even used the card once.

Don't close your oldest account once you "graduate" to better cards. This one surprised me — the length of your credit history matters, and closing your first card years later can actually shrink your average account age and ding your score, even if the card itself isn't useful anymore. Keep it open, maybe put one small recurring subscription on it, and forget about it.
Utilization matters more than people think. This is just the percentage of your available credit you're actually using. Even if you pay in full every month, carrying a balance that's, say, 80% of your limit right before the statement closes can hurt your score — because that's the snapshot that gets reported. A rough rule of thumb people use is staying under 30% of your limit, and lower is generally better.

It took me a while to realize this whole system isn't really about being good with money in some abstract sense — it's about generating a paper trail. Once you see it that way, the actual steps stop feeling mysterious.
