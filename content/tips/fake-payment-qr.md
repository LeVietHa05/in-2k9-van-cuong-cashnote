# How to Spot a Fake Payment QR Code (Before You Scan It) 

A few months ago I almost paid for a banh mi through a QR code taped over the vendor's actual QR code. I only noticed because the sticker was slightly crooked and the paper looked newer than everything else on the cart. That's it. That's the whole warning sign most of the time — something looks just a tiny bit off, and most of us scan anyway because we're in a hurry and QR payments feel too simple to go wrong.

I've been reading a lot about mobile payments for this blog, and it turns out "QR code overlay" scams — someone printing a fraud code and sticking it over a real one — are one of the most common frauds tied to the exact same QR payment boom I've been writing about all series. So here's what I've picked up on actually catching one before it gets your money.

Look at the sticker itself, not just the code. A legit merchant QR code is usually printed once and left alone for months — it's a little worn, maybe laminated, part of the setup. A scam overlay is new. It's often a slightly different size than the space it's covering, slightly misaligned, or printed on cheaper paper. If a QR code looks like it was added recently, that's worth a second look.

Check the name that pops up before you confirm. Every legitimate payment app shows you the recipient's name or business name before the money actually leaves your account — that's the one thing a fake sticker literally cannot fake, because it's your app reading the actual linked account, not the sticker. If you scan a code at a coffee shop and the name that shows up is a random personal name instead of the business, stop. Don't confirm. That single step catches almost every version of this scam.

Be extra careful with codes you didn't expect. A QR code someone hands you, one that appeared in a text message, or one taped somewhere unusual (like over an ATM or a parking meter) deserves way more suspicion than one that's just sitting at a checkout counter where it always is. Scammers love parking meters and public codes specifically because nobody expects a scam there.
If the amount is pre-filled and slightly off, don't just adjust and pay. Some scam codes pre-fill an amount that's close to but not exactly what you owe, betting you'll just edit the number instead of stopping to ask why it was wrong in the first place. If a pre-filled amount looks strange, that's a sign to just ask the vendor directly instead of fixing it yourself.

None of this takes more than a few extra seconds — it's really just training yourself to glance at the recipient name once before confirming, which most of us skip because we're moving fast. I've started doing it every time now, and honestly it's become as automatic as checking a crosswalk before stepping out.
