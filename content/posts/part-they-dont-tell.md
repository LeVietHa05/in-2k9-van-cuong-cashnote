So far this series has been kind of a highlight reel. M-Pesa, UPI, Pix, Vietnam's Mobile Money — all genuinely impressive stories about hundreds of millions of people getting a financial account for the first time in under two decades. But remember that four-layer thing from post one: Access, Usage, Quality, Welfare? Everything so far lives in layer one. This post is about what happens after someone actually gets the account — because it turns out "has an account" and "is actually included" are pretty different things.

## Knowing an account exists ≠ knowing how to use it

Here's a stat from Global Findex 2025 that's easy to miss in between the celebratory headlines: in a lot of lower-income countries, a large chunk of newly-banked people say they'd need help to actually use their account with confidence. Opening an account is fast now. Knowing what a PIN protects, spotting a scam transfer before you send it, or actually building a savings habit inside an app? Completely different skill, and nobody's really teaching it during sign-up.

This is why you see so many dormant accounts — millions of mobile money accounts get opened (sometimes because a government benefit program required it) and then just... sit there after the first transaction. An account nobody uses doesn't smooth out a bad month or build any savings. It's access without usage, and usage is where the actual benefit lives.

## The gender gap that just won't close

If there's one number from this whole series worth remembering, it's this: 55% of the globally unbanked are women. And it's not improving on its own.

GSMA's Mobile Gender Gap Report for 2026 found that women in lower-income countries are still about 12% less likely than men to even use mobile internet, leaving roughly 810 million women unconnected — more than two-thirds of them in South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, the exact same regions carrying most of the world's unbanked population. Smartphone ownership shows a similar 13% gap.

It actually gets worse once you zoom into mobile money specifically. GSMA found women in lower-income countries were 36% less likely than men to own a mobile money account in 2024, up from 30% in 2021. The gap is widening, not shrinking, even while overall adoption keeps growing. And the reasons cited aren't really about the tech at all — it's lack of awareness that the service is even relevant to them, limited digital skills, safety concerns using it in public, and social norms that limit women's independent access to money and phones in the first place.

This is exactly why Pix (from the last post) stands out — it's one of the only large systems where account growth showed no gender gap. That didn't happen by accident. Pix is free, mandatory for big banks to support, and simple enough that it doesn't need a whole literacy campaign to explain. Which is a pretty strong hint that design choices matter here just as much as infrastructure.

## Trust: the barrier infrastructure alone can't fix

Even when the phones, the coverage, and the payment rails all exist, people still hesitate if they don't trust the system. This shows up in a few very real ways:

Scams are everywhere once digital payment volume explodes — fake QR codes, phishing texts pretending to be a mobile money provider, agents quietly skimming a cut off a cash-out. One bad experience can undo months of someone slowly getting comfortable with digital money.

Agents matter more than the app. Systems like M-Pesa depend on a huge network of human agents to convert digital balance into cash. If your local agent keeps running out of cash, you stop trusting the whole system, no matter how good the app itself is.

Fees that feel unpredictable also quietly kill trust, even when they're technically cheap — especially if terms aren't clearly explained in someone's own language.

## The "last mile" that 4G coverage maps don't show you

It's tempting to think that once a country hits near-universal network coverage, the infrastructure problem is basically solved. In reality, a coverage map hides a lot — inconsistent electricity for charging a phone, patchy signal indoors in mountainous areas, and (going back to the gender gap) unequal access to the one phone a household actually owns. A stat like "99% of communities covered" can still leave a meaningful chunk of individual people effectively offline.

Where this leaves the "playbook"

Put all four of these together and it's pretty clear the next frontier for fintech isn't really about payment rails anymore — those got mostly solved by the systems in the last post. It's the less flashy stuff: literacy programs, fraud protection, agent network quality, and products designed specifically with the gender gap in mind.

Next post moves to a related but different problem: even people who do have a working payment account still usually can't get a loan, because there's no formal credit history for a bank to check. That's a whole different fix, using a completely different kind of data — and it's honestly the wildest part of this series.
