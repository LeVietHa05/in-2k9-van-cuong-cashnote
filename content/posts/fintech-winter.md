
If you had looked at the fintech industry in late 2021, it would have been difficult to imagine that anything could seriously slow its momentum. Venture capital continued flowing into startups at record levels, public market investors rewarded almost any company associated with digital finance, and founders were routinely encouraged to prioritize growth over profitability. The prevailing assumption seemed remarkably straightforward: financial services were undergoing an inevitable digital transformation, and the companies acquiring customers the fastest would eventually become the dominant institutions of the future. Looking back, what strikes me isn't that investors were optimistic about fintech itself. It's that many people began treating an unusually favorable funding environment as though it were a permanent feature of the industry rather than a temporary macroeconomic condition.

That's why I don't think the fintech winter of 2022-2023 should be understood primarily as a fintech story. It was, above all, a story about the price of capital. For more than a decade after the Global Financial Crisis, central banks maintained historically low interest rates while injecting enormous amounts of liquidity into financial markets through quantitative easing. The Zero Interest Rate Policy, or ZIRP, fundamentally changed the way investors evaluated growth companies because future earnings appeared extraordinarily valuable when discounted at exceptionally low rates. A startup promising profitability ten years into the future could still justify an enormous valuation if the opportunity cost of waiting was close to zero. Under those conditions, venture capital flourished, technology companies expanded aggressively, and fintech became one of the largest beneficiaries of an environment where capital was abundant and patience was inexpensive.

What I find particularly interesting is that the industry's business models did not suddenly become flawed in 2022. Digital payments remained one of the fastest-growing areas of finance, embedded finance continued expanding across e-commerce platforms, and consumers showed no meaningful desire to return to paper banking or cash-based transactions. The underlying direction of the industry remained remarkably consistent. What changed instead was the framework investors used to value those businesses. Once inflation accelerated after the pandemic, central banks responded with the fastest series of interest rate increases in decades, abruptly ending an era that many founders and investors had come to view as normal. Government bonds once again offered meaningful returns, borrowing became substantially more expensive, venture funding slowed, and the premium investors were willing to pay for distant future growth declined almost immediately. The destination hadn't changed, but the cost of getting there had increased dramatically.

The relationship between interest rates and startup valuations is often explained through discounted cash flow models, but I think the psychological dimension is just as important. Cheap capital doesn't simply lower financing costs; it changes incentives throughout the entire investment ecosystem. Investors become more willing to believe ambitious long-term narratives because waiting carries relatively little cost, founders become more comfortable sacrificing short-term profitability in pursuit of scale, and markets gradually begin rewarding customer growth, transaction volume, or market share more enthusiastically than sustainable earnings. None of those behaviors are inherently irrational when money is inexpensive. The problem arises when participants begin assuming those conditions will continue indefinitely. Once interest rates rise, the market doesn't suddenly become hostile toward innovation. It simply becomes far less willing to postpone difficult questions about business fundamentals.

Klarna illustrates this shift more clearly than almost any other company. At its peak, the Swedish Buy Now, Pay Later giant was valued at approximately $46 billion, making it one of Europe's most valuable private fintech companies. Less than a year later, a new funding round reduced that valuation to roughly $6.7 billion, a decline that quickly became symbolic of the broader correction sweeping through fintech. It's tempting to interpret that collapse as evidence that Klarna's business had fundamentally failed, but I don't think that's what happened. Consumers didn't suddenly abandon installment payments, nor did the company lose its ability to process transactions. What changed was the market's willingness to assign extraordinary value to future growth at a time when funding costs were rising, credit conditions were tightening, and investors had become far more sensitive to profitability. The valuation changed because the assumptions underpinning it changed.

A similar pattern emerged among publicly traded fintech companies. Firms such as PayPal, Block, Affirm, Robinhood, and Adyen all experienced substantial declines despite remaining influential businesses within digital finance. During the pandemic, investors had extrapolated unusually strong growth in e-commerce and digital payments far into the future, assuming that consumer behavior observed during lockdowns represented a permanent structural shift. As economies reopened and spending patterns normalized, growth naturally slowed, forcing markets to reassess expectations that had arguably become detached from reality. One lesson that finance repeatedly reinforces is that share prices rarely reflect whether a company is objectively good or bad. They reflect whether a company performs better or worse than investors already expected. Expectations matter just as much as execution, and periods of extraordinary optimism often leave very little room for disappointment.

Looking back, I think one of the more valuable lessons from the fintech winter is that technological innovation and financial valuation should never be treated as the same conversation. Throughout the previous decade, it became increasingly common to hear that profitability no longer mattered because platform businesses could always monetize later, another funding round was never too far away, and capturing market share was the only metric worth optimizing. That narrative wasn't entirely wrong, but it was heavily dependent on a macroeconomic environment characterized by exceptionally cheap capital. Once that environment disappeared, investors returned to much more traditional questions. Could customer acquisition costs eventually be recovered? Would margins improve as businesses matured? Did each additional user create long-term economic value, or merely increase operating expenses? Those questions had always been relevant. Markets simply stopped postponing them.

Ironically, I think the correction may have strengthened the fintech industry over the long run. Easy money has a tendency to disguise inefficient business models because almost every company can continue raising capital despite persistent losses. More disciplined financial conditions expose those weaknesses much earlier, encouraging founders to focus on operating leverage, sustainable unit economics, and capital efficiency instead of expansion at any cost. Many fintech companies emerged from the downturn leaner, more selective, and significantly more focused on profitability than they had been during the height of the funding boom. Investors, meanwhile, became noticeably more cautious about paying extraordinary valuations for growth that might not materialize for another decade. That adjustment was undoubtedly painful for founders and shareholders, but it arguably produced healthier incentives for the industry as a whole.

Perhaps that's the reason I don't see the fintech winter as evidence that fintech was overhyped or destined to fail. Digital payments continue gaining market share, embedded finance is becoming increasingly common, artificial intelligence is reshaping underwriting and fraud detection, and financial services are becoming progressively more integrated into everyday digital platforms. None of those long-term trends disappeared between 2022 and 2023. What disappeared was the assumption that every company participating in those trends automatically deserved an exceptional valuation regardless of its underlying economics. A business can genuinely transform an industry while still being a disappointing investment if expectations become detached from financial reality. In that sense, the fintech winter wasn't really a rejection of innovation at all. It was a reminder that even the most disruptive technologies remain subject to the same economic principles that have governed capital allocation for generations, and that sustainable value creation ultimately depends not only on building products people love, but also on building businesses capable of earning returns that justify the capital invested in them.
