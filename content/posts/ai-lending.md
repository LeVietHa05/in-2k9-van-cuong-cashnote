Picture Aimee, a food-truck owner in the Philippines. She's clearly good at her job — she's earning real income, and she's savvy enough to know she wants to expand. But she's never had a credit card or a formal loan, so there's no paper trail anywhere a bank could look at to decide if she'd pay one back. To a normal credit bureau, she's not "risky" — she's just invisible.

Now multiply Aimee by about 1.7 billion unbanked and underbanked people worldwide, representing something like $2.8 trillion in credit demand that traditional lenders literally have no way to evaluate. That's one of the biggest unsolved problems in global finance, and it's the topic of this post.

Everything covered so far in this series — M-Pesa, UPI, Pix — solved the payments problem. Credit is a harder problem, because the entire question a lender needs answered ("will this person actually pay me back?") has always been answered using exactly the kind of paperwork people like Aimee don't have. This post is about the companies that decided to answer that question anyway, using data nobody thought to use for lending before.

## The idea that sounds a little unhinged until you think about it

Here's the pitch: how you use your phone says a lot about whether you'll repay a loan — even with zero formal financial history. Two companies really proved this at scale.

Tala, founded in 2012, first launched in Kenya in 2014, builds its whole credit model off of roughly 10,000 data points pulled from someone's Android phone — device type, how apps get used, even how carefully someone fills out the loan application itself. They've since expanded into the Philippines, Mexico, and India, and by 2025 had served over 10 million customers with more than $6 billion in loans handed out — most of them first-time borrowers who'd never had access to formal credit before.

Branch, operating in Kenya, Nigeria, Tanzania, Mexico, and India, looks at handset details, SMS logs, GPS patterns, call history, and — once someone's borrowed before — their actual repayment record. Loans start small and grow as someone builds a track record, basically a credit ladder you climb without ever setting foot in a bank.

Zoom out across the whole sector and the impact is real: this kind of phone-based alternative scoring is estimated to have unlocked credit for more than 50 million people across Africa who never would've qualified under a traditional model — often starting with loans as tiny as $5 and scaling up from there based on how reliably someone pays it back.

## It's not just an "Africa/emerging markets" thing anymore

East Africa and South Asia are where this idea got proven, but it's now a genuinely global industry:

In the US, a company called Upstart uses AI trained on non-traditional data and claims to approve about 44% more borrowers than a traditional credit model would — with a meaningful chunk of that going to lower-income applicants specifically.

In India, companies like CreditVidya mine the transaction data UPI itself generates (yep, the same UPI from post two) to score people who have payment history but no formal credit history.

In the UK and EU, open banking regulation lets lenders see someone's actual bank cash flow directly, with their permission, instead of needing a traditional credit file at all — the UK alone had over 8 million active open banking users by mid-2025.

The pattern connecting all of this: the moment someone's financial life leaves any kind of digital trace — mobile money transactions, UPI payments, a linked bank account — that trace becomes a workable substitute for the paperwork lending used to require.

## The part that's worth being honest about

This isn't a free lunch, and I don't want to just gloss over it. Two real problems come with this approach.

It's hard to explain. A machine-learning model trained on thousands of behavioral signals is way harder to explain to someone who got rejected than a normal credit score built on a handful of transparent factors. "Why was I denied?" becomes a genuinely tough question to answer honestly.

It's a lot of personal data. GPS location, SMS logs, your entire contact list — that's a lot to hand over for a small loan, and privacy norms around this vary wildly by country and are still being actively fought over by regulators.

These aren't reasons to write the whole idea off — the inclusion numbers above are real — but they're exactly why this space is now getting serious regulatory attention around consent and transparency.

## Zooming out

Across this whole series so far: M-Pesa, UPI, and Pix solved payments by swapping a phone number or ID for a bank branch. Alternative credit scoring does the same trick, one layer deeper — swapping a digital behavioral trail for a paper credit history.

So here's the natural last question for this series: what would it take to build this substitution on purpose, at a national or even global level, instead of company by company? That's exactly what the final post looks at.
