
In my last post, I talked about the 1.3 billion people still stuck outside the financial system — and the fact that most of them already own a phone. So here's the natural next question: if the phones were already there, what actually took so long? And once someone figured it out, how did it work?

Turns out three countries independently solved this in three totally different ways. Let's go through all three, because the differences are honestly more interesting than the similarities.

## First, why couldn't banks just... do this already?

Before getting to the fixes, it's worth being clear about why regular banks struggled here in the first place. Picture a bank trying to serve someone in a village 40km from the nearest branch. They'd need to build or staff an actual branch (expensive, for tiny transaction amounts), collect full paperwork most informal workers just don't have, and charge fees that are nothing to a salaried employee but a real barrier to someone with income that swings by the season.

None of this is really a "banks are evil" story. It's a cost math problem — serving this customer just wasn't profitable under the old model. Fintech's real trick wasn't kindness, it was figuring out how to make the cost of serving one more person basically zero.

## Kenya's M-Pesa: the OG blueprint

Safaricom launched M-Pesa back in 2007. The idea was almost stupidly simple: send and receive money using a basic phone, cash it out through a network of local agents (basically shopkeepers doubling as human ATMs), no bank account required at any point. Before this, fewer than 1 in 5 adults in Kenya even had a bank account.

Fast forward to now and the numbers are honestly hard to believe:

M-Pesa has somewhere around 40–47 million active users in Kenya alone, out of a population of about 55 million. Kenya's overall mobile money penetration hit 91% by mid-2025 — one of the highest rates on the planet. Across the countries M-Pesa operates in (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Mozambique), it's now processing over $450 billion a year — and back in 2014, transactions in Kenya alone were already worth almost half the country's entire GDP.

It didn't stop at payments either — M-Pesa has expanded into savings, small loans, even investing. What started as "send money by text" quietly became a full financial ecosystem for people banks had basically given up on serving.

The one-line summary: swap the bank branch for a local agent, and swap the bank account for a phone number. That's it. That one substitution did more for financial inclusion in Kenya than decades of traditional banking expansion ever managed.

## India's UPI: same goal, completely different architecture

India didn't go the "one company builds a closed system" route. Instead, a government-owned body built something called UPI — an open rail that literally any bank or app (Google Pay, PhonePe, Paytm, whatever) can plug into.

UPI also didn't start from nothing — it was built on top of an earlier push called Jan Dhan Yojana, which had already opened around 558 million bank accounts for previously unbanked Indians, mostly through simplified ID verification linked to India's national biometric ID system, Aadhaar. Those accounts were mostly sitting there unused, though — UPI is what made them actually usable, day to day, for buying vegetables from a street cart or paying an auto-rickshaw.

The scale this reached is honestly kind of absurd. UPI processed 18.39 billion transactions in a single month (June 2025), worth roughly $290 billion. It now makes up 84–85% of all digital retail payments in India. And it's become a genuine export — Peru, Namibia, and several other countries have signed on to build their own UPI-style systems.

The lesson from India, versus Kenya, is slightly different: it wasn't really about removing the branch requirement (that was step one). It was interoperability — letting every bank, every app, and every tiny merchant transact on one shared, government-run rail — that turned out to be just as powerful.

## Brazil's Pix: the top-down approach

Brazil's central bank launched Pix in November 2020, right in the middle of the pandemic, partly to get emergency aid to unbanked people fast. Unlike the other two, this wasn't built by a company or grown organically — the central bank basically ordered the country's biggest banks to support it, and made it completely free for regular people.

The results speak for themselves: before Pix, less than half of Brazilians had a bank account. By 2025, that number is around 90% — and interestingly, with basically no gender gap, which (spoiler for post 3) is not the norm globally.

76% of Brazilians now say Pix is their go-to payment method, ahead of both debit cards and cash. In 2024 alone, Brazil recorded 68.7 billion Pix transactions — a 52% jump from the year before — worth close to $5 trillion. One study even estimated Pix delivers something like $380 per person per quarter in pure convenience/cost savings, just from not needing expensive wire transfers anymore.

Brazil's version of the playbook adds one more piece the other two didn't lean on as hard: a legal mandate. Force the big banks to participate, make it free, and you solve the cost barrier and the "everyone's on a different app" problem in one move.

## So what's the actual pattern here?

Three totally different starting points — a private telecom in Kenya, a government-built public rail in India, a central-bank mandate in Brazil — but they all attacked the exact same three problems:

No branch needed — everything happens on a phone. Simplified verification — a phone number or national ID replaces a mountain of paperwork. Free or nearly free — no minimum balance, no punishing fees.

Vietnam's own Mobile Money rollout, launched in 2021 through the three big telecoms, is a smaller-scale version of the exact same idea — link a payment account to your SIM card, no bank account or smartphone needed. By early 2025 it had reached almost 10.4 million accounts, with 72% of users in rural, mountainous, or border areas — basically the exact demographic Global Findex says is underserved globally. It's a good reminder that this playbook isn't limited to a handful of headline-grabbing mega-countries.

## But wait — is "having an account" actually the win condition?

Not quite, and this is the honest caveat to end on. Everything above is basically measuring layers one and two from the first post — Access and Usage. It doesn't tell us whether any of this actually improved people's lives in a deeper way (that's layers three and four — Quality and Welfare).

Global Findex 2025 actually flags this directly: in a lot of lower-income countries, a big chunk of newly-banked people say they'd still need help to use their account confidently. Having a phone-based account is necessary, but apparently it's not sufficient on its own.

Which is exactly what the next post digs into — what's still standing in the way even after someone technically has an account.
