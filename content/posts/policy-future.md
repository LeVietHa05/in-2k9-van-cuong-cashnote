This series kicked off with one number: 1.3 billion adults still outside the financial system, most of them already holding a phone. Over the last four posts, we've gone through how fintech companies and, in a few cases, entire governments turned that phone into a substitute for a bank branch, a credit history, or both. This last post looks forward — what are the next bets being placed, and which ones actually look built for real inclusion instead of just extra convenience for people who were already banked?

## India is straight-up exporting its playbook

Probably the biggest shift happening right now isn't a new piece of tech at all — it's a new way of thinking about the tech that already worked. India packaged the whole system behind UPI's success (a national digital ID called Aadhaar, UPI for payments, and a document-sharing platform called DigiLocker) into something it calls Digital Public Infrastructure, and it's actively giving the blueprint away.

As of early 2026, India has signed cooperation agreements with roughly 23–24 countries to adopt pieces of this, and UPI itself already runs as a live payment system in more than eight countries — UAE, Singapore, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan, Mauritius, Qatar, even parts of France. Trinidad and Tobago just became the first Caribbean country to adopt it, Kenya adopted the DigiLocker piece, and — kind of a fun detail for this blog specifically — Vietnam is on the list of countries that signed a cooperation agreement with India on this.

Why does this matter more than any single app? Because it turns "build inclusive financial infrastructure from scratch" from a multi-year, hugely expensive national project into something closer to adopting an open-source template. India's own officials describe this as shifting from "DPI 1.0" (get people included — identity, basic payments) to "DPI 2.0" (use that base for actual productivity — credit, farming, health, small business lending).

## CBDCs: huge ambition, honestly mixed results

Central bank digital currencies — a digital version of a country's actual currency, issued directly by the central bank, not a cryptocurrency — get pitched constantly as a financial inclusion tool. The idea is a CBDC wallet could be even simpler than a regular mobile money account. The real-world track record so far is pretty mixed:

Nigeria's eNaira has barely gotten traction, which pushed the central bank to launch a bank-backed stablecoin called cNGN as a kind of course correction. Ghana's eCedi is leaning on merchant QR codes to try to replicate what made M-Pesa and Pix work. Peru's CBDC pilot has crossed 100,000 users — small, but a real base. And Brazil's Drex project (building on Pix's success) actually walked back its blockchain-based design over privacy and scaling concerns, and is now focused on more boring back-office stuff instead of consumer inclusion.

Honest takeaway: CBDCs are still an open question. The systems that actually moved the needle so far — M-Pesa, UPI, Pix — worked because of simple design choices (agent networks, interoperability, being free and mandatory), not because of the specific technology underneath. Nobody's proven yet that a CBDC beats a well-designed mobile money system for inclusion specifically, even though a lot of governments are clearly betting it will.

## Open banking: turning "no credit history" into "here's my cash flow"

The UK and EU are running a different experiment worth watching. Instead of building one national payment system, regulation there just forces banks to let customers share their own transaction data with any licensed third party, if they want to. It's already a real ecosystem — the UK alone had over 8 million active users by mid-2025 — and it solves basically the same problem as alternative credit scoring from post four, just for people who already have a bank account but no legible credit history yet.

## Interoperability: the boring word that decides who actually wins

If there's one theme running under every single system in this series, it's this: the ones that actually worked at scale — M-Pesa's agent network, UPI's open rail any bank can plug into, Pix's mandate covering every major Brazilian bank — all cracked interoperability early. The ones struggling right now, including a few CBDC pilots, are still figuring out how to not become a walled garden that only works if you already use one specific bank or app.

Groups like the IMF and Atlantic Council have both flagged this as basically the central design question for the next decade of financial inclusion policy — without shared standards, a messy landscape of competing digital currencies could actually recreate the same exclusion problem, just in digital form, with better marketing.


## Wrapping up

Looking back across all five of these posts, the throughline is honestly pretty simple even as the tech keeps getting fancier: every genuinely successful financial inclusion story — from an agent in a Nairobi kiosk in 2007 to an AI model scoring a food-truck owner in Manila today — worked by substituting something people already have (a phone number, a digital ID, a transaction history) for something they don't (a nearby bank branch, a paper credit file, a national ID system that actually reaches them). Whatever comes next — DPI exports, CBDCs, open finance regulation — will probably get judged by the same question this whole series keeps circling back to: does it actually reach the 1.3 billion people still on the outside, or does it just make life a bit more convenient for the people who were already in?
